The Diaphragmatic Check

A 90-second self-test built on a finding from the OR: most experienced anesthesia clinicians have lost conscious contact with the one muscle they manage all day. Let's find out where you stand — one hand on your chest, one on your belly.

Step 1 · At rest

Set your hands, then breathe normally.

The protocol:
  1. One palm flat on your upper chest, one palm flat on your belly, just above the navel.
  2. Breathe the way you always do — don't perform it. Take three easy breaths.
  3. Feel which hand travels more.

Which hand moved more?

Step 2 · Under load

Now add a little cognitive pressure.

Keep your hands exactly where they are. Solve this in your head while you keep breathing — no pausing your breath to think:

Count down from 100 by 7s — out loud, as fast as you can.

Do it for a full three breaths. This is the OR twist: a task steals your attention, and your breath quietly changes without you. Now — which hand moved more?

What you just found

Diaphragmatic blindness

In an informal test of 30 anesthesia professionals on their breaks, 28 could not engage the diaphragm on command — they breathed with accessory muscles (scalenes, sternocleidomastoid, upper intercostals) instead. The two who could? A singer and a trumpet player.

It isn't a fitness problem. The diaphragm runs on brainstem autopilot through the phrenic nerve from your first breath, so it has almost no voluntary cortical map — until you build one. With 30 seconds of coaching, all 30 clinicians reached a 10-second diaphragmatic inhale. The capacity was always there; the awareness wasn't.

Step 3 · The 30-second re-teach

Find the diaphragm on purpose.

  1. Keep the belly hand on. Let the chest hand stay still — that's the goal, a quiet chest.
  2. Inhale slowly through the nose and aim the air low — imagine sending it under the belly hand so that hand rises first.
  3. Let the belly gently push your hand out on the inhale; let it fall on the exhale. Chest last, not first.
  4. Try one slow nasal inhale toward 8–10 seconds. Certified diaphragmatic breathing runs 8+ seconds; accessory-only breathing tops out around 4–5.

Awareness before technique: you cannot train a breath pattern you cannot feel. That's the whole reason this check comes first.
